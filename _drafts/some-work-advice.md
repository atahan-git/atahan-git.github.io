I love giving and taking advice. Last week I was talking to my brother about his work problems. After listening to him for 2 hours straight, I had 5 things to say to him. Aaand he was like "wow these are good, you should write a book", sooooo, here's a blogpost about them.

These are work advice I learned over my 3 years of working. That might not seem a lot of time, but most of them came in the form of me first hearing the advice from a senior/staff engineer, not entirely believing the advice, making a mistake that the advice was meant for, then me starting following the advice myself too. And one or two I figured out by observing what the long timers are doing, getting confused why they are doing seemingly useless things, then finding out the exact reason why they are doing it.

Let's get into it!

# 1. Document everything, summary and lots and lots of random references, WITH DATES
This is one of first advice I ever heard at the job, in the context of trying for a promotion. But it is applicable in a much wider context than that. I'm sure you've heard a version of this one too. The hard part for me to really understand what this is for and what do I really need to put in here.

The weak version of this is just keeping a log of what you've done with links. That is how I started it. Just an excel sheet with all the work I've done with Jira/gdocs links. That was mostly useless. I kept sooo much in there, but come promotion season almost none of it was useful.

The strong version of this I learned over time is to keep a weekly "journal". The idea is, nobody but you will see this. Make it fun! Complain about someone doing something stupid. Write your failures. Put in screenshots from slack about something nice somebody said to you. Put in confidential slides from the company all hands talking about how many users the project you worked on got. Take a screenshot of people's reaction to it. This journal is for your eyes only! Put down your thoughts about that impromptu meeting with all the important people in the company.

But most importantly, write down DATES!

Oh and also, make it a Thing™. Put down 1h before you head home on friday to fill your journal about what happened that week. Protect that time. Don't Skip It.

This doc serves several purposes. Number 1 is of course for promotion prep. It's very hand to have those positive interactions and numbers about a project when it's time to prep that promo doc. Even the negative stuff is useful, because people remember it. You need to put stuff to counter them. When the reviewer asks that random engineer you worked with for a week 2 months ago and they say "oh, so and so, yeah I remember. He failed that thing", you need to have something in that doc to explain why it wasn't necessarily your fault, or at least some nice little story of how you learned from it. Even better if after making that doc you go and talk with that engineer and be like "Hey, we had that bad experience. Help me understand how I can do better next time". It's a little bit playing into their egos for sure, but also I learned a lot of good lessons that way! Most people love giving advice...

Number 2 is to cover your ass. When things aren't going well and several people are giving you jobs, they don't always communicate. In their view it might be that the "little task" they gave you 3 weeks ago is still not done. They don't know 3 other people have given you "high priority" tasks in the meantime. When they come asking what you've been doing all this time, you can hit them with "on 16th of may X person told me to this and it's important, then on 23rd of may Y person told me to this other thing". Replying with dates have the effect of making people pause and consider it as fact. If you just say "yeah but X and Y has been giving me a bunch of, uh, work? Sorry", then they won't take it as seriously. People misremember. Maybe they vaguely heard of that thing happening, but their version of it was "X and Y gave him 1 day tasks!" while your versions is "they each gave me stuff that takes weeks!". You need to provide dates and details so they are more likely to trust your Data™ than their own memory. Because if its your memory vs their memory, you know which one is going to win.

The most important part of this is making it easy for you. 3 sentences every week is 100x more valuable than an essay every month. When you say "I keep logs every day" it creates respect for the process. Because in the back of everyone's mind is "I wish I kept logs like that". Give them the logs. That 1 hour you spend every week will save weeks of pain and interpersonal conflict down the line. 

At the end of the day, your memory is fallible. YOU will also misremember things. You might not even remember that the 1 hour task _actually took you 2 weeks_. Which leads us to...

# 2. Over estimate time needed, do not overwork if you dont want to. say "they might do it faster, if you want me this is the time"
This is a weird one, but it works soooo well. There are a couple of things at play here. So lets start with a story...

When I first started working, I was having intense impostor syndrome. On the outside, I was a fresh intern and later a fresh hire. They would give me work, and I would finish them all on time. Praise all around. But on the inside, I was conflicted. I was working for like 1-3 hours a day. Tasks that seemed like would take 1 day would baloon to 1 week. I would forget then remember things, causing delays on how much I expected the work to take. But the promotions and the praise kept coming. I did some research. "The average office worker is truly productive for only about 2 hours and 53 minutes out of a standard 8-hour workday". I observed myself. Sure, I was on my desk working for 1-3 hours + meetings, but then I would take a walk at 11pm and think about the ticket for an hour. I would figure out the solution while taking a shower.

Work takes longer than you think. Knowledge work often happens in the background. You might see the 1 hour you struggled at your desk. 5 minutes going over some text. Then 2 days later, you do the damn thing in 5 minutes. Why can't I just spend 10 minutes and be done with it? Because you needed those 2 days. You needed to sleep on it. You needed to see the sun shining behing the leaves to get the inspiration. You needed to watch that random youtube video that caught your eye _for some reason_ to get there.

Unless you know the exact steps you need to take, overestimate. If you know the exact steps you need to take, but you haven't done this thing in the past week, overestimate. I find that 2-10x overestimation is the sweet spot. Also, most work has a long tail, here's an article about estimating software engineering work [Why software projects take longer than you think: a statistical model](https://erikbern.com/2019/04/15/why-software-projects-take-longer-than-you-think-a-statistical-model.html).

The second part of this is overestimating the work liberally _if you don't want to do the work_. Often times I get tickets that I really don't want to do. Sometimes it's because it will touch a hairy part of the codebase, causing lots of minor problems and edge cases. It makes my brain hurt. Other times I disagree that we really need this. The engineering cost don't seem to be worth the user benefit. Sometimes it's plain annoying work. I'm an engineer, I don't want to run after people or write a random ass doc. Don't say no, give your estimate. "(hmm, a 2 hour task), Yeah I can do that, it will take 2 weeks.". This serves a couple of purposes. 

One and the most important one is that it will often really "take" 2 weeks! There is a cost to doing annoying tasks. It will leave you _drained_. You really might need 2 weeks to go back up to full power.

Second is that a lot of the time the manager will look at the time, and reconsider if we really need this thing. Talks might happen. Maybe it will get cut, maybe it will transform to be a better task. Let them do _their_ work. Other times they will say "but so and so said this is a 2 hour task!". Just reply "They might do it faster, but if you want me to do it, this it my estimate". Let them make the choice. Cut? Transform? Give it to the other guy? Be careful though, you are not the villain. You are just stating the facts. And you _need_ to be stating the facts. Don't go overboard. Don't say it will take 100x longer. But within 2-10x, it's anyone's guess. And if the person giving you the task doesn't know all the things to know about this particular thing (they almost never know) then they have to take your word for it. Remember, you hold expertise. 

Third is sometimes you will actually get the work. But it's great, because now you have 2 weeks on it instead of 2 hours! Take your time. Do the housekeeping tasks you've been avoiding. Touch grass. You earned it. You are doing a task that _needs to be done_ and _no one else wants to do_. And if it truly takes only 1 week and you are sitting without anything to do? You just did something in half the time! A hero!

This one took me a while to learn. Because it's so tied with my ego. I _want_ to do it fast. I want to take only 2 hours. I want to say yes to everything. I want to avoid conflict. But be aware, people will remember when you miss the deadline. People won't remember the 2 minute conversation when you told them it will take longer than they think. 

Never lie about this though. If you do this _every time_ then people will start remembering it. You are working within the bounds of uncertainty everyone has. You are just playing the uncertainty in your advantage, you aren't just straight up lying. Take this as more of an adjustment of your internal estimator. NOT taking the answer it gives you, and adding extra. Over time, you will just become a better estimator. Because here's the thing, everybody is playing by the same rules. The other person who said it will take 2 hours either will also take 2 weeks, or they are pulling overtime and they will soon burn themselves out. 

There is also a point to be made about overworking. Working till the night. It never ends well. The average person can keep that up for a couple days at most. After that, you might seem to be pulling the hours, but you will get slower. The task that takes you 1-3 hours will now expand to take 8 hours. You will make mistakes. Sure, you delivered it "on time", but if you add all the cleanup work afterwards, it ends up taking the full 2 weeks anyways! You will end up pulling long days eventually, but use them sparingly. Be very aware of the debt they come with. And remember, you are an employee. Don't sell your sanity to a company if they aren't paying for it.

Annoying last minute tasks also have a tendency to make all the other work late. That's another reason to overestimate. Work in the time it will take to do all your other tasks while doing this annoying work. Work in the time that the random unexpected email you will get into your estimate. And the topic of competing tasks leads us to the next point.

# 3. When competing stuff is there, make them talk with each other to figure it out. Say "can do it, but these things will be late"
This is a common thing in especially smaller companies where there is a lot to be done in all the different teams, and those team leads don't often talk to each other. You might find yourself in a situation where you have 5 different 2 hour tasks all due today because everyone giving you those tasks only see their own single tasks and "you have 8 hours in the day, just do this 5 minute thing (that will actually take 2 hours)!"

In this case, your Journal will come in handy. Pull those dates. "X gave me this task I need by Y date, and there are these 3 other things to be done.". And hit them with the most important part: "_I_ don't know which one is the higher priority. Can you tell me?". Make them important. Make them decide what gets delivered late. This can go in two ways:

Sometimes they don't know the priority either. They might send you on a chase to ask the other people if their thing can be postponed. Many times I found myself in a situation where I'm just passing along comments about how each thing is oh so important and can't the other person's thing wait? Other times I successfully take myself out of the conversation, and just make the two leads talk to each other and figure out the details. Either way, suddenly it's not "Can _you_ do it?" and "Which task is a better use of this guy's time?". And it makes those leads feel important too! Now instead of looking at the ceiling, they need to _prioritize_ and _generate alignment_ and _talk to important people_ and _get things done_.

Sometimes they know the priority, or at least they think they do. They might say "Oh I know that other thing, our thing is more important.". Leave a paper trail. Don't point fingers, you are a guy of facts. Just send a quick email "hey, so and so told me to this, so this other thing I promised will now take another week" and cc them both. If that feels wrong, just log it in your journal. "on the 3rd of may X asked me to this, so Y will be late". Then when they come asking about Y you can give the facts. "Oh so and so told me it's okay this was late". Then they can figure it out between themselves. 

Really, this is part of your implied job description. It's your _job_ to make this visible. You aren't deciding what's the most important above you, you are giving them the information they need to decide that for you. You are deciding what is important below you, but there is always someone above you. Even if you are the project lead, other people will want you to do things. Even if you are the CEO, you have shareholders. Use your best judgement, but if you are getting two competing requests, make sure they talk to each other.

Funnily enough this also works with friends. Two people invited you to something the same day? Tell them! Sometimes one of them will decide "oh that other thing is more interesting!" and join you. Or at least, they can go "yeaaah, I would've gone to that other thing too". Or sometimes one of the things will get postponed so all the friends can attend both things! No reason the same thing shouldn't apply at work. 

This also works well because often you Don't Know all the things people higher up know. Sometimes that "highest priority" task is _actually less priority than the competing task_. Let them do their job, so they can let you do yours. Go with best intentions. You are uncovering the real work priority. You aren't trying to skip work. Help them help you. And on the point of how best to help them help you, we get to the next point.

# 4. When delivering decisions, go simple,

Whenever there is a task with unclear ways to solve it, I make a quick doc. Sometimes the doc lives only in my brain, sometimes it's a short slack message. I always put:
1. A sentence about what is the problem
2. A sentence about what we are trying to do
3. ~3 solutions with time estimates, pros and cons
4. My personal recommended solution
5. Long explanation after the executive summary

Eg it might look like:
> Marketing thinks a minority of users will have a bad sentiment about this product.
> 
> So, they want to add a little form to collect "feedback" after the experience ends.
> 
> There are a couple ways to do this:
> 1. Reuse an existing form. Should take about a week, but it won't be perfect.
> 2. Make a totally new form. Probably will take 2-3 weeks, but we can put exactly what we want.
> 3. Add to the experience description that they can send their feedback to an external feedback system. Would only take 2 days, but most users will probably skip this.
>
> I recommend option 1, as it will get us the most benefit with the least time spent.
>
> [half a page each, describing of some technical details about each option and why they are taking the time they are taking]

This sort of thing works GREAT. The busy manager can just tell you "oh lets do your recommendation" and you are set! Especially great if you were avoiding some of the other options. Other times, maybe they don't have 2 weeks and they need the form NOW, sooo instead of telling you to "Can't you just do this? It will take only 5 minutes I'm sure!", they can pick the easy option. Yet other times a lively discussion happens about how a different person can get it done faster or how marketing really really wants option 2 and can't we postpone these other things? Yet other times all options kinda suck and the entire thing gets cut.

No matter what happens, you just made a bunch of people feel Smart because now they have Information and Options and oh wow they picked the best one! And if they don't pick the thing you recommended and something catches fire, hey, you told them so. Of course never say that part out loud, but they will know. Just mention "yeah we had these other options including option 2 that I recommended. Should we go back to that one?". And of course to be able to do that effectively, always make note of these decision pieces in your Journal.

When this comes in the form of a slack message, the "technical details" part should be hidden in the thread. When this a doc about something big, you can use a couple of sentences instead of single sentences to describe. But the 5 things MUST fit in a single page, if not just half a page. You want the executive summary to be read. Nobody will probably read the long description in the following pages, but you also HAVE to have them. One sentence is hard to believe. One sentence followed by 2 pages you really don't want to read is a lot easier to believe. Those 2 following pages needs to have substance, but they don't need to be complete or anything. If they are ever needed you can expand upon them. Their job is to make them look at the 3 pages, look at the cool "Executive Summary", and do the mental math to read the short bit and believe your word. And if your word is ever questioned, you just go "as I explain in the later section, there are these things that make it take that long". 


All these will cover your bases and avoid "who said what" situations and answer "what have you been doing all this time instead of delivering the one thing I asked from you?". But sometimes your manager just kinda sucks. In that case, you will need the next part.

# 5. Make an internal note on what will make you quit, if these keep happening, look around. Look around even if they don't happen.

This is probably the single biggest thing that kept me from quitting. The average Software Engineer changes jobs every 2-3 years. The average person changes jobs every 4 years. But to get passionate at a job, to love it, to get really damn good at it, you might need more like 15 years on it (more on that in the book [So Good They Can't Ignore You](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/13525945-so-good-they-can-t-ignore-you)). There is real benefit to working somewhere for a long time. I've been making games for about 12 years now, and I love it. I've been at my current job for only ~3 years, but I consider quitting almost every year. They are going to change my manager again later this year. It will be my 4th manager. I got moved around teams. Every time it takes month to get used to the new working environment. Every time there is new conflict I never had to deal with before. But I'm slowly seeing the benefits. I can see how the gears of the company is working. I can predict how long a task will take if it takes 2-3 teams to coordinate. I can guess which of the 3 options we will go with when I make a decision summary. I'm getting rapport with more people. 

I had a lot of problems during my first manager change. The new one did so many things differently. We argued a lot. Just a month in, he told me:

> This is the work. Maybe it's time to look around.

And I did. The industry sucks. I talk to my friends who are also Software Engineers. I hear the overtime they pull in. I hear the job hopping they do. I talk to my friends in other industries. The work is different, yet the problems are often the same. I applied to a couple jobs. Didn't hear back from majority. I did some interviews. I saw that their working environment isn't so different. 

At the end of the day, I love my coworkers. I learned to work with that manager, and he ended up being the best manager I've had so far. I still have that internal note. I still look around. It made me love my job more. It made the current issue seem smaller, more manageable. 

Your mileage may vary. Maybe your workplace truly sucks. Maybe you are better suited to a different industry. Or maybe you just need to learn, to grow, and in 10 more years this will become your passion.
